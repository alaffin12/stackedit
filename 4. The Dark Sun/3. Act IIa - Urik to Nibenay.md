# Into the Desert
Because he has a vested interest in the success of the players (as they are to retrieve for him the Lens of Rajaat, a powerful relic from a bygone era which will make Hamanu the most powerful Sorcerer-King save perhaps Borys himself) is sending a caravan with the players to ensure their survival in the harsh wastes of Athas.  This will be broken into two parts - the trip from Urik to Nibenay, which will be a slower moving caravan featuring a full complement of slaves; and the trip from Nibenay to the Pristine Tower, which will see the slaves and most of the caravan left behind in favour of fast moving crodlus and a company of Urikite Templars for protection.

Players will have to manage food and water supplies, as well as the morale of their travelling companions.  Running out of food and water in the desert is deadly, but keeping the spirits of their companions high is also important - low morale can result in calamity out on the dunes.  Templar morale starts at 4, while slave morale starts at 5.  The caravans will also encounter some of the harsh realities of life on Athas.

For every day on the road players will have to make various choices and rolls to see how they survive the day itself.

## Daily Checks
Every day the party will have to make checks to survive on the road.  One check is made during the midday short rest (no one with any sense travels during the warmest part of the day in the desert) and one is made at night during the long rest.  

**Midday Check:** DC 20 Consitution saving throw against the sapping heat of the desert sun.  Players wearing medium or heavy armor have disadvantage on this save.  Players who have resistance to fire damage make this save automatically.  On a failure the player gains a level of exhaustion.  

**Midnight Check:** DC 15 Constitution saving throw against the cold of the desert night.  Players who are resistant to cold damage automatically make this save.  On a failure the players does not gain the benefits of a long rest.

## Outposts and Forts
There are five outcroppings of civilization (for some definition of civilization) along the way to the Pristine Tower - Silver Spring, Shazlim, Fort Isus, Nibenay and Fort Inix.  Each of these communities will offer the party opportunities to resupply before heading out.  However, with the exception of Nibenay - which is itself a major city - prices are high, resources are low and everyone is out to make a few bits.  For each 'town' I will have a quick list of the price of entry (if applicable), the price of food/water (as well as any limits), the price of slaves to buy/sell, the markup on phb goods.  Additionally, while equipment that makes sense will be readily available in these places, exotic equipment might not be, so there will be a percentage indicating how likely any item is to appear in the town's markets 

## The Journey
### Day 3 (morning): The Warden of Salt
> It is your third day in this heat-soaked land and while Sereth assures you that the caravan is getting close to somewhere at least nominally 'alive' you have a hard time believing anything lives out in this sandy hell.  Even the gentle breeze from the movement of the wagon feels like heat blasting against you skin.  
>
> You pass along a small canyon, the wheels of your caravan crunch as they break through the thin crust of crystal that cover the ground here.  Salt.  You imagine this was once a river or an inlet of some sort, that became a salt-flat when the oceans dried.  It doesn't matter really - the desert is littered with places like this.  Places where all the life has been sucked from a land, leaving it nothing but grit and heat.  The animals don't seem to mind.  Maybe the slaves, who often drop off the wagon to attend to some problem or another.  But you try really hard to not think too much about their plight.
>
> As you move along you hear a shout from one of the Outriders.  You don't understand it - the Templars all seem to insist on speaking in Urikite - but it quickly gets Sereth to his feet, looking around.  Soon he sees what the warning was about - an old man, treading barefoot and scraggly along the salt-basin.  *"A defiler..."* Sereth grumbles.  Before you can ask Sereth explains: *"Defilers - you have heard the word before, no?  A magus who drains life from the world in order to fuel his magic.  This one is old.  Likely mad and dangerous.  Do you want me to order the Templars to kill him?"*

Players can attack the Defiler outright, or can talk to him.  If they choose to talk:

> The old man - his bones decrepit, his body misshapen - looks up at you.  "I am the Salt Warden.  I serve a penance here to pay for the crimes of my youth.  Those who pass this trail must pay a price, not of silver or iron.  A price of yesterday and tomorrow.  A memory of joy to sustain me in this barren waste.  Tell me - will you pay, or will you allow your soul to shrivel?"

If a player donates a memory of joy they lose all their memories of that event, but more importantly: -2 templar morale (should have let the slaves do it), +1 slave morale (happy they didn't have to do it).

If a templar donates a memory of joy: -3 templar morale (should have let the slaves do it, betrayal), +1 slave morale (happy they did not have to do it)

If a slave donates a memory of joy: -3 slave morale (didn't want to do it)

If the party refuses or tries to kill the defiler - a fight breaks out, as the Defiler summons dark, salt based creatures to assist him.  A battle ensues, which we theatre of the mind. > The settlement of Silver Spring appears almost by accident upon the desert horizon, a scattering of canvas and hide pavilions strung between crooked posts and weathered palms. Unlike the stone-walled villages of the city-states, nothing here feels permanent; every structure looks as though it could be packed up within the hour. The "streets" are little more than trampled sand between clusters of tents, with the occasional rope strung overhead to give shade. A thin line of stakes marks what passes for a palisade, not so much a defense as a reminder of boundaries. Even the oasis itself is half-hidden, a shallow pool ringed with crude woven mats to keep the sand from slipping into it, constantly tended as if it might vanish at the next strong wind.

>The elves of the Silver Hand move about with the restless air of people who have not yet convinced themselves they belong in one place. Traders haggle beside low fires, their goods spread out on rugs rather than displayed in stalls. Children dart between tents with the same light-footed speed as if they were on the open dunes. Everything is transient: the smells of roasting erdlu, the flutter of dyed cloth, the music of bone flutes carried on the wind. It feels less like a village and more like a camp paused mid-journey, waiting for the signal to move again. Yet beneath this fragility lies something harder to place—an unease, perhaps, or a defiance—as though Silver Spring exists not because it should, but because its people refuse to give it up.

>The trail to Shazlim clings to the edge of a vast, sunken wasteland, where the earth drops away into an expanse of shimmering heat and cracked stone. The settlement itself crouches on a rocky outcrop above this desolation, its walls little more than a token barrier of mudbrick and timber. From a distance, the place looks fragile, temporary, as though a strong wind might tear it loose and send it tumbling into the bowl below. The gate, narrow and half-collapsed, is watched by guards in patchwork gear who eye travelers with suspicion as much as caution. Even from outside, the murmur of voices, the clatter of markets, and the occasional barked order of authority carries on the hot wind, giving the impression of a town straining to assert itself against its precarious perch.

>Within, Shazlim presents two faces. Its center boasts shaded villas and inns designed for the wealthy of nearby cities, who come here to play at hardship with wine and stories of the “untamed frontier.” Lanterns and silks flutter in courtyards, musicians strum for guests reclining on cushions, and servants hurry to refill cups with precious water or sour wine. But just beyond those inner streets, the settlement devolves into narrow alleys of dust and stone, where the common folk scrape by selling trinkets, hauling goods, or bending to whatever labor nobles demand. Justice here is swift and merciless—stocks in the square, whips in the open—part punishment, part entertainment for the visiting elite. Shazlim is a settlement at odds with itself, clinging to the edge of survival and spectacle in equal measure.

#### Notes
* Shazlim is currently in the midst of a crisis, as water is leaking from their cistern.  As a result food/water is in short supply, and only ten units may be purchased by the caravan.

#### The Justice of the Water Lords
> As you move through the town, with a large part of your caravan following you come to a square that is clearly more for the locals than the tourists.  It is small, crowded and filled with noise and smoke.  Villagers gather, pressing close, silent and unmoving in their pale linen shawls.  They watch as three tall, willowy figures and one massive one move through the square, a loud metallic clang following their steps.
> 
> The large figure is the source of the sound, as the chains binding the too-tight metal shackles he wears clatter against the pressed stone pathways.  The half-giant trudges forward, dragging as much as stepping, moving like a man half-buried
>  
> Ahead of him stride his jailers - garbed in veils and copper jewelry, swaying as they walk with the deliberate grace of ritual. One casts handfuls of salt into the air from a bone bowl; another lets precious water drip from a cracked ladle onto the stones. Each drop hisses into the dry earth.  A third walks with a copped-capped staff - slamming it on the ground in time with the half-giant's laboured steps.  The three, and the crowd around him shout and jeer.
> 
> Sereth leans over to you, knowing you likely do not understand what you are seeing.  *"They say he is apostate.  That he stole water that was not his.  The ones walking before him - the three veiled figures - are the Water Lords, the rulers of this great town..."* Sereth is obviously being sarcastic calling it a great town - he clearly doesn't think much of it.
> 
> The half-giant breaks his silence with a sudden roar, his voice raw with desperation. The language is unfamiliar - guttural and coarse - but his anguish is clear.
> 
> *“The half-giant says he is innocent..."* Sereth continues *"...he says he found a crack in the lower cistern.  says he was trying to mend it.  Trying to protect the city, its water.  Says he saved the town's water supply from spilling into the soil.  Claims he sealed it with resin and clay.”*
> 
> The half-giant jerks forward again, snarling past the dust that villagers toss at his feet. He shouts once more, gesturing with his shackled arms toward the central platform, where a wide iron grate waits sealed—the mouth of a cistern, but it does not look like the main one.  
> 
>  *“He’s swearing on his ancestor’s bones he never stole a drop."* Sereth continues. *“Says the Lords came later. That they saw the wet clay on his hands and didn’t ask questions.  The people here - they do not speak his language.  They do not understand him.”*
>  
>  The crowd begins to murmur, repeating a phrase in rhythm with the slow beat of the Water Lords' staff.  *"They chant..."* Sereth says *"...the water was not his.  The water was not his.  The water was not his."*  The crowd swells around the square’s edge, as the Water Lords take the platform. One raises their hand and the chant dies. Another speaks aloud in the local tongue, a sharp, declarative sentence.
> 
> Sereth nods slightly, translating under his breath: *"The Water Lord calls for silence.  He says they have tried the young half giant, found him guilty.  The punishment for the crime is drowning...”*  Sereth shakes his head *"...a custom here.  Not drowning as you would know it - folk from wet lands would not even think of it.  The cistern they stand on it old.  The water below is maybe a few inches deep.  With muck and filth beneath it.  They will lower the half-giant into it, upside down, until his face is buried in it. They will leave him there until he 'drowns'."*  Sereth shakes his head.  *"Useless barbarity.  If you will kill a man then kill him.  If you wish to send a message, then do not bury it out of sight."*
> 
>The half-giant howls again, but there’s no defiance left—only despair. He stumbles. The guards jerk him upright. The crowd does not flinch.  Around the square, the villagers avert their eyes. Some spit into the dust. A few children weep quietly, but no one interferes.
>
> Sereth’s voice is dry as the wind: *“This is justice in Shazlim. The water is sacred. The Water Lords are its voice. Even kindness can be a crime - especially if you are an outsider.”*

This is a loose interaction, players can simply ignore it and move on.  However any action - even ignoring it - can have consequences:

* The easiest path is to ignore the problem.  Shazlim is its own city state, and the caravan have no true authority here (other that that which comes from the point of their blades).  The Templars seem unbothered by the harsh justice of the Water Lords, bar Sereth (who is more upset at the wastefulness of the act than the actual act).  However the slaves are bothered by the injustice of it, and if the players do not intervene they will take a -1 penalty to their morale.
* If the party intervene physically they save the half-giant as there is nothing in Shazlim capable of providing meaningful resistance to the party.  However they will not be able to stay in the town, meaning they will not be able to buy food/water or gear.  The Templars will also be upset at being dragged into pointless combat, and will take a -1 to their morale.
* If the party try to reason or threaten their way to success evaluate their efforts versus the method used and set a DC for success (minimum should be around 15 - justice, even harsh and misled justice - is not easily swayed).  If the party invoke Hamanu then set the DC lower or give advantage as appropriate, but the Templar's take a -1 to their morale for their Sorcerer-King being invoked in such a petty way.  If they succeed, the half-giant is spared and the slaves will get a +1 to their morale.  If they fail, the half-giant is killed but the slaves appreciate the effort.  If they use intimidation in particular prices of all things sold to them should increase by 25% to reflect the townsfolk not being happy with their actions.
* If the players use some kind of trickery or other plan then adjudicate based on what is presented.  If they do it well enough they can reduce costs by 10% and increase the amount of food/water available by 10 units.  Saving the half-giant will also result in +1 to slave morale.

> Fort Isus appears suddenly from the wastes, less a fortress than a wound carved into the desert floor. From the outside it looks unimpressive—a shallow ditch surrounded by low, sun-baked ramparts of packed stone and clay no higher than a crodlu’s neck. But as the caravan draws nearer, the subtle depth of the place becomes clear: the walls are only the lip of a much deeper hollow dug into the earth. A wide ramp descends into the fort, where the bustling heart of the post lies well below the desert wind. The air cools noticeably as the party approaches the gate, and even the sound of the dunes seems to fall away, replaced by the noise of traders shouting, wheels grinding against hard stone, and the chatter of soldiers stationed beneath the shade of the ramparts.

>Inside, Fort Isus feels more like a canyon town than a proper stronghold. House M’ke banners hang above narrow markets pressed against the walls, while makeshift awnings stretch between buildings to create shadowed lanes. The fort’s guardians are unmistakable: templars from three rival cities, each in their distinct livery—Nibenay’s veiled enforcers, wearing tabards of black featuring a purple serpent, Urik’s stern watchmen in white wearing a golden lion, and the gaudy but cruel-eyed agents of Raam dressed in golden with a blue mellikot for an animal. They patrol the fort in uneasy cooperation, their authority overlapping in a constant game of silent rivalry. Here, merchants hawk overpriced water and spice, mercenaries loiter for coin, and everyone pretends not to notice the constant measuring glances between templar patrols. Fort Isus thrives not because it is rich or strong, but because it is useful—an oasis of order in the shifting wilds, where even bitter enemies set aside bloodshed in the name of trade.

-  **Side with the Nibenese Templar**: -2 templar morale (betrayal), +1 slave morale (admire the lawful nature of the party.  The next day the Nibenese Templars provide the party with 10 extra food/water supplies as a thank you.
- **Try to de-escalate**: Requires a reasonable DC 15 check.  If the party succeeds the Raamish templar thanks the players for helping them out of a tight spot and gifts the players 10 extra food/water supplies.  Everyone else grumbles and goes away - no morale change.  If they fail no one is happy and they take -1 to slave and templar morale.
